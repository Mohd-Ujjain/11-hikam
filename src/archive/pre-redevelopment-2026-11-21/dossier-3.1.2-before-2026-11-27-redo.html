CThe One Thread to Follow

Follow one question: what does each view commit us to? Move from worldviews to religions’ accounts of the human predicament, then compare three answers about God and the world. Agreement on what is ultimate does not decide how it acts or what could have been otherwise.

§IPhilosophy: Build and Assess a Worldview

This section answers: why is identifying a worldview's categories only the beginning of philosophy?

We inherit views about what exists, what we can know, and what is worth pursuing. Philosophy makes these commitments explicit, asks how they fit together and tests the arguments for them. Yandell offers a working account rather than a definition everyone must accept: philosophy constructs and assesses categorial systems. A category is a basic concept whose meaning cannot be analysed into other concepts within the system; different systems may take different concepts as basic.

SOURCE DEPTHCategories and argumentsOne source detail · optional

Categories and arguments

A category is basic within the system being considered. Other concepts can be made clearer against it. Identifying the basic concepts still leaves the distinct task of evaluating the reasons for accepting that system.

Keith E. Yandell, Philosophy of Religion: A Contemporary Introduction, pp. 3–4, 13–14.

One can work from a general picture down to a particular issue, or begin with a particular issue and discover the wider commitments that an answer carries. Neither route exempts a claim from assessment. Understanding a philosophical position requires grasping the reasons its supporters can offer, not merely listing its conclusions.

SOURCE DEPTHTwo routes into philosophyOne source detail · optional

Two routes into philosophy

Starting with a broad view and starting with a local problem can lead to the same commitments. The particularist still uses reasoning with more general implications. Neither route grants a claim immunity from rational assessment.

Keith E. Yandell, Philosophy of Religion: A Contemporary Introduction, pp. 13–14.

§IIReligion: Diagnosis, Cure and the Life of a Tradition

This section answers: why does the nature of the alleged problem determine what could count as a cure?

Yandell asks what a tradition thinks is fundamentally wrong with human life and what could put it right. The cure makes sense only in light of the diagnosis. Forgiveness addresses sin; it does not replace insight where ignorance is the basic difficulty. A tradition gives its answer through doctrines, practices, institutions and rituals, not by making a theoretical claim in isolation.

SOURCE DEPTHThe introduction’s roadmapOne source detail · optional

The introduction’s roadmap

The introduction previews kinds of religion and religious experience, the importance of doctrine, arguments for and against monotheism, three nonmonotheistic traditions, religion and morality, and faith and reason. Yandell distinguishes acceptance of propositions from the trust in God that accompanies faith in monotheism. His aim is to let the student practise assessment, not simply to catalogue old and new opinions.

Keith E. Yandell, Philosophy of Religion: A Contemporary Introduction, pp. 3–7.

A functional definition identifies what religion does; a substantial definition specifies what a religious tradition includes. Neither a generic social function nor belief in a creator alone identifies the range of traditions at issue. Yandell's account includes a diagnosis and cure, their expression in a community's life, and an account of what is ultimately real. Belief and practice give one another content and direction.

SOURCE DEPTHA neutral definition without neutralising religionOne source detail · optional

A neutral definition without neutralising religion

Yandell contrasts reductive definitions, including the opiate example, with an account that prejudges no tradition’s truth. A tradition interprets the world and human place, proposes a way of living, and embodies both in rituals, institutions and practices. Social-scientific study of practices and philosophical study of doctrine can complement each other.

Keith E. Yandell, Philosophy of Religion: A Contemporary Introduction, pp. 16–18.

§IIIObjectivity and Religious Disagreement

This section answers: does disagreement make reasonable assessment impossible?

There is no single test of truth that answers every question. Whether evidence is relevant depends on the claim. Having a preference does not rule out objectivity if one can follow reasons against it. Yandell treats a blanket denial of objectivity as self-undermining, while allowing that objective inquiry is hard and that success is not guaranteed.

Respecting a tradition means taking its claims seriously. Where Jainism affirms the same person endures into enlightenment and Theravāda denies that an enduring person survives in that sense, both accounts cannot be true in the same respect. This does not mean traditions never agree, that all religious claims are false, or that seeing a contradiction tells us which side is right. Philosophy of religion examines concepts, positions and arguments rather than resolving disagreements by definition.

SOURCE DEPTHHow personal identity changes the remedyOne source detail · optional

How personal identity changes the remedy

In the Theravāda view presented, a set of momentary states succeeds another; strictly, no enduring subject acquires or loses a property. In Yandell’s Jain example a person is an enduring, indestructible self-conscious being. His comparison considers both argument and the traditions’ appeals to enlightenment experience. In the Introduction he also asks whether part of the force of the problem of evil might presuppose the high value of persons, as in the image-of-God account. He presents that suggestion tentatively.

Keith E. Yandell, Philosophy of Religion: A Contemporary Introduction, pp. 5–6, 14–15.

★Two Obstacles to Objectivity

Yandell rejects the idea that a comparison can objectively select and interpret the evidence, yet cannot objectively assess it. He also rejects the stronger claim that objectivity is nowhere possible. His example is a sentence in English claiming that nothing said in English is true: if the sentence were true, it would itself be false. A defensible assessment takes work; a catalogue of disagreement is not the same work.

✕Common Mistake — One Method or No Method

Different claims need different evidence. A historical claim, an inference about what exists and a report of experience cannot simply be assessed by the same procedure.

Yandell does not present his own method as an inventory of views. He argues that religious claims can be rationally assessed and that the authoritative texts of traditions show they do make claims. Taking the claims seriously can be a form of respect. It also allows the author to reach substantive judgements without pretending to offer a complete philosophical system.

CWhat Counts as a Reason?

The introduction sets up questions rather than delivering all its later arguments. It points to the existence of things that could have been absent, the intelligible order of the world, moral standards not of our making, and reports of experiences of God as considerations urged for monotheism. It names evil as the most influential consideration against it. Asking whether evil and God can coexist is different from asking whether the evils we observe count against God's existence.

§IVThree Monotheistic Conceptions

This section answers: how can thinkers agree that God is ultimate yet disagree about the world and history?

Generic philosophical monotheism identifies God as necessarily independent, self-conscious, distinct from and not dependent on the world, and highest in value. Necessary independence says what God cannot depend on; it does not yet decide whether God's existence is itself necessary. Yandell then compares three models, not an exhaustive classification.

SOURCE DEPTHWhat strong and weak providence specifyOne source detail · optional

What strong and weak providence specify

The distinction is not simply how frequently God intervenes. Strong providence includes particular public events produced other than only through private events. In weak providence, apart from the creation of the world, public results come about only by way of private events such as revelations. He mentions Neo-Platonism as another possible model.

Keith E. Yandell, Philosophy of Religion: A Contemporary Introduction, pp. 85, 89–90.

The Greek deity is immutable, knows necessary truths but not contingent events, and neither creates nor intervenes. Its perfection remains a standard of value. The Semitic model makes the world's existence contingent on God's choice. God sustains and acts in history, so particular events matter to the traditions' truth claims. The Hindu model combines a beginningless world with continued dependence on God: having no first moment is not the same as having to exist.

SOURCE DEPTHDependence, history and valueOne source detail · optional

Dependence, history and value

The Greek deity is the perfect standard against which mutable things can better or worse realise the possibilities of their kind. Yandell also discusses why a chain of composites or a stock of simple units still invites an explanation of contingent existence. Semitic traditions link doctrines to particular persons or events; Yandell illustrates Hindu manifestations by an appearance of Krishna linked to a temple tradition.

Keith E. Yandell, Philosophy of Religion: A Contemporary Introduction, pp. 86–90.

★Why the Model Matters to the Argument from Evil

In the Greek model, a deity who cannot know contingent particulars or act in history cannot intervene to avert a particular evil. That model cannot offer divine help, forgiveness or answers to prayer in the providential sense either. With creation or providence, those goods are possible and the question about evil arises. Yandell does not say the objection must succeed.

§VNecessity, Contingency and the NN Principle

This section answers: why does judging that God exists differ from judging how God exists?

A first-order claim says that something is so. A modal claim tells us whether it must be so, cannot be so, or could have gone either way. Tim says frogs exist and Tom says they do not. Tex and Tim both accept frogs, but Tex thinks a frogless world is impossible while Tim does not. That disagreement concerns modality rather than the observed animals.

SOURCE DEPTHThe frog argument in two levelsOne source detail · optional

The frog argument in two levels

Tim and Tom disagree about a fact, while Tex and Tim disagree about whether that fact could have been otherwise. Tex takes a frogless world to be contradictory. Yandell uses the contrast to separate disputes about God’s existence from disputes about its logical status.

Keith E. Yandell, Philosophy of Religion: A Contemporary Introduction, pp. 91–92.

Yandell's NN principle concerns the status of modal claims: true modal propositions are necessarily true, false ones necessarily false. This applies at higher orders too. It does not decide whether God exists or which modal description is right. A contingent monotheist can consistently say that God exists, could have failed to exist, and could not depend on anything if existing.

SOURCE DEPTHNN does not yield a proof of GodOne source detail · optional

NN does not yield a proof of God

Yandell treats modal propositions about first-order claims separately from the claims they describe. Within each pair of alternatives, the false modal account is necessarily false. This does not reveal which account is false. A denial that God must exist is also not an assertion that God necessarily does not exist.

Keith E. Yandell, Philosophy of Religion: A Contemporary Introduction, pp. 91–93.

DRead the Negation Carefully

“Not necessarily true” is not “necessarily false.” If a claim is contingent, both its truth and its falsity are logically possible. Here “possible” means free from contradiction, not that we are unsure or that a probability has been assigned. NN rules out contingent truth or falsity for the modal proposition itself, not for the first-order proposition it describes.

§VIWhat Remains Open

Monotheists typically distinguish God from dependent persons and physical things, but this leaves questions about souls, moral freedom and rebellion, and what natural laws explain. A law might describe a regularity, state a deeper or more basic relation, or be probabilistic rather than exceptionless. Which account is right affects later reasoning about creation, determinism, freedom and responsibility. The supplied extract opens these questions rather than settling them.

SOURCE DEPTHWhy natural laws enter the discussionOne source detail · optional

Why natural laws enter the discussion

Yandell contrasts necessary conditionals, laws expressing things’ dispositions, and approximate generalisations; he then notes the case for genuinely probabilistic basic laws. His conditional and essence examples allow more than one consistent world to be created. The point here is to prepare for later issues of freedom and responsibility, not decide them.

Keith E. Yandell, Philosophy of Religion: A Contemporary Introduction, pp. 94–95.

Chapter Summary

For Keith E. Yandell, philosophy constructs and assesses categorial systems; philosophy of religion does that work on religious concepts, claims and reasons. Religions link diagnoses and cures to different accounts of ultimate reality. Objectivity requires responsiveness to evidence, not the absence of commitments. Compare the Greek, Semitic and Hindu models by dependence, time and providence. Distinguish God's necessary independence from necessary existence; the NN principle governs modal truth and falsity without deciding which form of monotheism or atheism is correct.
