CCentral Thesis

Dermot Moran presents twentieth-century philosophy as an unusually varied, turbulent, and productive field. Philosophers developed powerful new methods, particularly in logic and the study of language, while continuing to debate older questions about knowledge, time, consciousness, ethics, and reality. Philosophy also became overwhelmingly university-based, increasingly open to women, and divided into specialized fields. Moran argues that we cannot understand this legacy through a simple story of progress or by treating analytic and Continental philosophy as wholly separate traditions. We must examine what continued, what changed, and how the developments influenced one another.

§IProfessionalization & Diversification of the Discipline

This section answers: How did the organization of philosophy change, and how did women and new fields reshape it?

During the twentieth century, philosophy became an overwhelmingly university-based profession. More philosophers worked in academic institutions, and independent scholars became increasingly rare. Moran notes a few exceptions, including dissident thinkers outside established institutions.

What Does It Mean to Assess This Century?

Moran begins by adapting Benedetto Croce's question about Hegel: what is still living, and what is now dead, in twentieth-century philosophy? He is not asking for a list of famous names or a verdict that one school defeated another. He wants to identify genuine achievements, continuing problems, dead ends, and new possibilities without allowing loyalty to one tradition to distort the history. The century produced so much work, while also living through such extraordinary upheaval, that its philosophical legacy remains difficult to absorb.

Public Influence and Quiet Research

Academic influence and public influence were not always the same thing. Moran suggests that Marx's ideas, as taken up by Lenin, Mao, and their followers, may have had the largest practical impact through the political systems influenced by them. In public life, Sartre and Russell opposed the Vietnam War; Dewey campaigned for progressive education; Arendt wrote about the Eichmann trial; and Peter Singer's Animal Liberation prompted public debate about our treatment of animals. Moran also mentions A. J. Ayer's widely influential Language, Truth and Logic, the public appeal of existentialism, and the popularity of philosophical books and biographies.

At the same time, philosophers such as Wittgenstein, Kripke, Husserl, Levinas, and Rawls often worked away from public debate while making major contributions to specialized research. Public prominence and philosophical originality therefore cannot be measured by the same yardstick.

The century's academic institutions also depended on long-standing channels for research and debate. Journals such as Mind, The Monist, Philosophical Review, Kant-Studien, and the Journal of Philosophy continued to develop across the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Moran also stresses the growth of the history of philosophy as an independent scholarly field: critical editions, new translations, and research on ancient and medieval thinkers greatly enlarged what philosophers knew about their own tradition.

The Entrance of Women

An important change was the growing presence of women philosophers. They challenged barriers to academic careers, contributed to established debates, and helped open new areas of inquiry.

These examples demonstrate both the wider access to philosophical institutions and the growing range of their work.

The Rise of Specialized Disciplines

Philosophical study became more specialized. Growing fields included mathematical logic, meta-ethics (questions about the meaning and basis of moral judgments), philosophy of language, philosophy of mind, and inquiries into gender, embodiment, and the environment. Moran associates Arne Naess with ecosophy, a philosophy of ecological harmony. Some areas became distinct disciplines; others developed out of older debates.

Older Questions, New Tools

Some twentieth-century achievements were strikingly new, while other discussions continued questions inherited from much earlier philosophy. Moran particularly distinguishes technical progress—where a formal method or distinction can become more precise—from questions about moral truth and human life, where comparable progress is harder to establish.

For example, a sophisticated logical system does not by itself settle whether a moral judgment such as “slavery has always been wrong” is objectively true. This distinction between technical refinement and unsettled philosophical judgment is central to Moran's assessment.

Naturalism and Its Transcendental Critics

Naturalism grew across the century. In broad terms, naturalistic approaches seek to understand philosophical questions through the world studied by the natural sciences. Moran recalls Roy Wood Sellars's 1922 statement, “We are all naturalists now,” as a sign of this trend—not as proof that all philosophers agreed.

Husserl objected that the natural sciences alone cannot account for the conditions under which something is known as objective. In his Philosophy as a Rigorous Science (1911), he treated naturalism as a threat to philosophy understood as a rigorous inquiry. Later transcendental thinkers likewise argued that normative questions—questions about validity, justification, or what ought to be—cannot simply be reduced to descriptions of natural events. Moran describes a continuing tension here, with renewed transcendental philosophy especially after the 1970s.

DTwo Terms — Naturalism and Transcendental Inquiry

Naturalism: an approach that gives a central explanatory role to the natural world and the sciences. Transcendental inquiry: an inquiry into the conditions that make knowledge and objectivity possible, including the knowing subject's role. These are broad orientations, not two perfectly uniform schools.

DDefinition — The "Long Century"

Calling it a "long century" does not mean it contained extra calendar years. Moran uses the expression to convey the scale of what happened: world wars, genocide, political upheaval, scientific change, and globalization. Its intellectual legacy was so extensive that it remained difficult to assess after the century ended.

§IIThe Analytic–Continental Divide and Its Shared Roots

This section answers: Why were analytic and Continental philosophy treated as opposing traditions, and what did they still share?

One of the best-known divisions of the century was between analytic and Continental philosophy. The labels suggest two opposing methods, but Moran argues that this picture is too simple: the traditions grew from related nineteenth-century debates and often addressed the same questions.

The Nineteenth-Century Background

The divide did not begin with two entirely separate worlds. On the European Continent, neo-Kantians responded to Hegelian speculation by calling for a return “back to Kant” (zurück zu Kant) and for a careful account of the conditions of scientific knowledge. In Britain, by contrast, Hegelian and neo-Hegelian idealism remained influential through figures such as F. H. Bradley and McTaggart. Russell's criticism of Bradley particularly concerned whether relations between things were real rather than reducible to the internal nature of their terms.

The history did not end in a simple victory over idealism. Moran traces a later revival of interest in Hegel in France, Germany, Britain, and the United States. Hegelian themes reappeared even within analytic philosophy in the work of John McDowell and Robert Brandom, and in Critical Theory. Thus a tradition can reject an earlier thinker and later return to that thinker's questions.

Why the Labels Are Difficult

“Continental” is largely a name applied from outside the tradition, sometimes from a British point of view. Some philosophers prefer “European philosophy,” but that title is also awkward: Frege, Carnap, and Wittgenstein were Europeans, while philosophers working in the so-called Continental manner also taught and wrote in the United States. Even “Anglo-American” overlooks important German-speaking contributors to analytic philosophy. Moran notes the suggestion “Anglo-German” to recognize that history.

Neither school has a single fixed method. Both examine language, skepticism, relativism, science, and the possibility of knowledge. On one side, Frege and Russell developed logical analysis; on the other, Husserl's descriptive phenomenology developed into Heidegger's more interpretive approach and later deconstruction. Those developments make the labels historically useful but philosophically imperfect.

DDefinition — Psychologism

Psychologism is the attempt to explain logical truths through the processes of human psychology. Frege, Husserl, Russell, and others rejected the reduction of logic to how people happen to think. Their shared concern was to explain how logical statements can have a validity that does not depend merely on an individual's mental habits.

★High-Yield Point — Shared Origins

Despite the later division, early analytic philosophy and phenomenology shared important starting points. Thinkers in both movements questioned speculative idealism and rejected psychologism—the attempt to explain logical truths simply as facts about how minds think. They also faced common questions about science, meaning, and knowledge. Their methods developed differently, but they did not begin as unrelated enterprises.

A Shared Problem: Skepticism and Relativism

Both traditions repeatedly addressed skepticism (whether knowledge is possible) and relativism (whether truth or rationality depends on a historical, social, or conceptual framework). Moran points to changing views in figures as different as Dilthey, Nietzsche, Foucault, Quine, Kuhn, Wittgenstein, and Putnam. The particular theories differ, so the term “relativism” should not be treated as if it names one position accepted by all of them.

An especially important divergence concerns the role of formal logic. Frege and Russell developed the power of mathematical symbolism for logical analysis. Husserl, though deeply concerned with logic, gave priority to a transcendental question: how conscious acts achieve objective validity. This methodological difference helps explain why related beginnings could produce different philosophical traditions.

§IIIPhilosophical Beginnings Around 1900

This section answers: Why are Husserl, Frege, Wittgenstein, and Heidegger important to Moran's account?

Around 1900, important publications opened new directions in philosophy. Moran pays close attention to Husserl and Frege, while recognizing the immense later influence of Wittgenstein and Heidegger. These thinkers did not all follow the same programme.

The Turning Point Around 1900

Moran identifies several events around the turn of the century, not a single founding moment. Moore's “The Nature of Judgment” (1899) presented propositions as objective and analyzable rather than merely mental. Freud's Interpretation of Dreams (1899) inaugurated psychoanalysis. Nietzsche died in 1900, while his writings continued to raise questions about truth, power, values, and historical interpretation. Husserl's Prolegomena to Pure Logic appeared in 1900. At the 1900 International Congress of Philosophy in Paris, Russell met Giuseppe Peano; Peano's exact approach helped turn Russell towards mathematical logic.

1. Edmund Husserl and Phenomenology

Husserl called his Logical Investigations (1900/1901) his "breakthrough work." It challenged the idea that logic can be reduced to psychological processes. It also asked how consciousness comes to grasp meanings and other ideal objects.

  • The Goal: carefully describe experiences of thinking, knowing, and understanding meaning, rather than beginning with speculative theories.
  • The Principle — Intuition: take something as evident only when it genuinely presents itself in direct understanding or experience. For Husserl, this direct grasp is central to philosophical evidence.

Two related points matter in Husserl's account. First, logical forms, numbers, and meanings are not merely private mental occurrences. Second, we gain access to such meanings through identifiable acts of consciousness—intending, recognizing, and judging. Husserl's subsequent turn to the transcendental ego deepened his question about how consciousness can achieve knowledge of objective meanings. The phenomenological movement that grew around his work included Adolf Reinach, Alexander Pfänder, Johannes Daubert, Moritz Geiger, and Max Scheler.

🧭
Clarifying Image — Husserl's Method

"To the things themselves" (die Sachen selbst) means: describe what is actually given, as it is given, before imposing a theory. As a simple illustration, describe how a flower appears to you before claiming that a theory already tells you everything about that experience. The flower is an illustration, not an example supplied by Moran or a complete account of Husserl's method.

2. Gottlob Frege and the Logical Foundation

Frege's work became foundational for analytic philosophy even though its wider influence was largely recognized later. He helped philosophers examine the logical form of statements rather than simply following their surface grammar.

DDefinition — Sense & Reference (Sinn / Bedeutung)

Frege distinguished a term's sense (Sinn)—the way something is presented—from its reference (Bedeutung)—what the term refers to. This distinction helped explain how language can convey thought.

⭐
Clarifying Example

As an illustration, "the Morning Star" and "the Evening Star" both refer to Venus: they have the same reference, but present it in different ways and therefore have different senses. Learning that the two names refer to the same planet tells us something we might not already know. Moran discusses Frege's distinction, but does not supply this particular example in the reading.

  • The Context Principle: consider a word's meaning in the context of a complete sentence, rather than in isolation.
  • Impact: Frege distinguished logical structure from grammatical appearance and resisted treating thoughts as private mental events. Moran links his work to later advances in language, logic, and metaphysics.

Frege also helped reshape quantificational logic: formal ways of expressing claims about individuals, relations, and what exists. Moran notes the consequences for philosophy of language, philosophy of science, mind, and analytic metaphysics. Frege's context principle treats the sentence as central to determining a word's meaning. Moran also records that Husserl studied and annotated Frege's works—a concrete reminder that these two later-separated traditions had direct intellectual contact.

Moran does not present Frege's intellectual legacy without qualification: he also records Frege's association with a right-wing philosophical society and the anti-Catholic and antisemitic sentiments in his diary. Those historical facts belong to the source's portrait of Frege, alongside his technical achievements.

"Instead of following grammar blindly, the logician ought rather to see his task as that of freeing us from the fetters of language." — Gottlob Frege

Nietzsche and Freud: Influential, but at Different Times

Moran considers whether Nietzsche should be seen as the defining philosophical voice of the century. Nietzsche's writings anticipate problems of truth, power, inherited values, interpretation, and the writing of history. Foucault was a particularly direct successor. Yet Moran notes an important historical qualification: Richard Rorty thought Nietzsche became fully integrated into academic philosophy chiefly through Heidegger; before then, his influence had often been literary.

Freud also entered philosophy unevenly. Psychoanalysis helped shape some work associated with Horkheimer, Adorno, and Marcuse, but early academic philosophers often remained skeptical. Sartre questioned Freud's conception of the unconscious, while Popper challenged psychoanalysis's claim to scientific status on grounds of falsifiability. Ricoeur helped renew French philosophical engagement with Freud; from the 1960s, Lacan, Derrida, Foucault, Deleuze, Guattari, Kristeva, and others increasingly took his work into philosophical debate.

★Distinction — Presence at the Century's Beginning Is Not Immediate Influence

Nietzsche and Freud are landmarks at the turn of the century, but their reception was gradual. Moran therefore asks us to distinguish when an idea or book appeared from when it became influential within academic philosophy. His case for Husserl and Frege rests on their formative methodological influence as well as on their dates.

§IVAnalytic Philosophy and Its Internal Critiques

This section answers: How did analytic philosophers revise the methods and assumptions of their own tradition?

Analytic philosophy did not remain fixed after its early successes. Later philosophers questioned some of its key assumptions while continuing to work within a recognizable tradition. Moran presents these changes as evidence of continuity and self-criticism.

The Analytic Tradition Was Never Just One Method

Russell initially described analysis as finding the simpler elements that make up a complex. Yet his own work also sought broad accounts of space, time, causation, matter, and relations. His 1905 paper “On Denoting” became a model of analysis because a sentence's grammatical appearance can conceal its logical structure. G. E. Moore's defense of common sense offered another important style of philosophical argument. The early Wittgenstein's Tractatus combined the ideal of logical clarification with an ambitious, systematic account of how propositions relate to the world.

The Vienna Circle gave science and logical verification a particularly prominent role. Carnap and Ayer sought to distinguish genuinely meaningful propositions from pseudo-problems arising from misleading language. Their outlook did not exhaust analytic philosophy. In Britain, ordinary-language philosophers such as Gilbert Ryle and J. L. Austin investigated how ordinary expressions work; P. F. Strawson, still recognizably analytic, challenged Russell's account of descriptions. Moran thus traces a living tradition that repeatedly changed its own assumptions.

What Quine and Davidson Actually Rejected

Quine's “Two Dogmas of Empiricism” (1951) has two targets. First, he rejects the supposed sharp distinction between analytic statements (true only by meaning) and synthetic statements (true because of facts). Second, he rejects reductionism: the claim that each meaningful factual statement can be individually reduced to immediate experience for verification.

In place of isolated testing, Quine proposes a connected web of knowledge. Statements meet experience together, as a body; even apparently central principles can in principle be reconsidered. Davidson's 1974 essay then challenges another division: the idea of a conceptual scheme organizing a separate, unorganized content of sensation. Moran presents Davidson's “third dogma” as a deliberate continuation of Quine's critique, not as an identical argument.

DThree Distinctions Challenged by Quine and Davidson

Analytic / synthetic: meaning-based truth versus fact-based truth. Reductionism: each factual statement supposedly has its own test in immediate experience. Scheme / content: an organizing conceptual framework supposedly stands apart from the sensory material it organizes. Quine attacks the first two; Davidson challenges the third.

🧸
Memory Trick — ELI10: The Analytic Evolution

Imagine trying to understand a game. Russell's analysis examines its pieces and their relationships. Logical positivists ask which claims have clear significance through logic or possible experience. Quine says our beliefs work together like connected rules: experience tests the system, not each claim entirely alone. Davidson questions whether we can divide our organizing ideas from completely unorganized experience underneath them. This game is an explanatory analogy, not one used by Moran.

Quine's critique did not simply replace one official analytic doctrine with a new permanent system. Moran highlights a change in the self-understanding of analytic philosophy: it gradually came to be recognized as a historical tradition in which major ideas could be challenged without abandoning careful conceptual analysis. Moran also notes that Ayer, despite rejecting metaphysics in his published programme, later recommended studying the history of philosophy as a good introduction to the subject.

✕Common Mistake

Keep the two criticisms separate. Quine challenges the analytic/synthetic distinction: the supposed sharp line between truths based on meaning and truths based on facts. He also rejects testing every meaningful claim in isolation. Davidson challenges the scheme/content distinction: the picture of an organizing conceptual framework applied to independent, unorganized data. Davidson develops his criticism in conversation with Quine, but their targets are not identical.

§VThe World Wars, Migration & the Meaning of Europe

This section answers: How did the World Wars, especially Nazism and the Second World War, change academic philosophy?

The World Wars changed the lives of philosophers and the institutions where they worked. Moran distinguishes the intellectual disruption of the First World War from the far greater displacement caused by Nazism and the Second World War.

  • Changing Centres: Europe was central to academic philosophy in the first half of the century. After 1945, the centre of philosophical activity shifted increasingly towards the United States.
  • Forced Migration: Nazi persecution drove scholars abroad. Vienna Circle members such as Carnap and Feigl, and thinkers associated with the Frankfurt School, were among the Europeans who moved into American academic life; some emigrants first went elsewhere.
  • Heidegger and Nazism: Moran calls Heidegger's participation in Nazism a "great betrayal" of Husserl and the academy. His political commitments are part of Moran's assessment of his legacy, even while Moran acknowledges his continuing philosophical influence.

Two Wars, Different Effects

Moran treats the Second World War as the most important historical and sociological event for the institutional practice of twentieth-century philosophy. The First World War brought terrible human and political consequences, but it did not threaten the continued existence of philosophy in the same institutional way. It loosened the hold of some nineteenth-century schools, while some students became attracted to more “concrete” philosophies such as phenomenology, existentialism, and philosophies of life.

Russell's opposition to the First World War changed his career: he lost his Trinity College position in 1916 and was imprisoned in 1918. His friend Wittgenstein served in the Austrian army on the opposing side. Moran also records how other German academics responded differently, including patriotic writing and support for Germany's war effort. This contrast illustrates why war affected philosophers both personally and intellectually.

Why “Europe” Became a Philosophical Question

Moran asks us not to treat “Europe” as a fixed or merely geographical label. The centres of philosophical research changed, and philosophers disagreed over what the European intellectual heritage should mean after political catastrophe. Husserl's Crisis of the European Sciences (1936) argues that modern mathematical science had encouraged a division between measurable facts and human meanings or values. He proposed reflective philosophy (Besinnung) as a way to address that loss of meaning. Moran also records criticism of the exclusions in Husserl's own understanding of European rationality, including his 1935 Vienna lecture.

Jan Patočka wrote of “post-Europe” and drew on the Greek concern for justice and the “care of the soul.” Jacques Derrida spoke of a “Europe of hope” that would retain Enlightenment values while acknowledging Europe's colonial, totalitarian, and genocidal past. Jürgen Habermas considered how Europe's critical reflection on its history might help identities reach beyond old national divisions. The source presents these as different attempts to understand Europe's meaning, not as one shared definition.

Traditions Change as They Travel

Moran also shows why the location and language of a philosophical movement matter. Phenomenology changed as it entered France: Sartre connected Husserl with French Cartesian themes, and Levinas interpreted Husserl partly through Bergson. Heidegger's Being and Time was discussed in Germany from 1927 and in France from the 1930s, but it was not translated into English until 1962. Similarly, ideas from Cambridge did not immediately cross into Oxford. The work of Polish logicians, including Tarski, remained important, even as post-war western discussions often absorbed their contributions into an American intellectual setting.

The result is a warning for historical study: philosophers are sometimes grouped under traditions they did not themselves fully accept. The same work can be interpreted differently when it crosses institutions, languages, and countries.

Politics Returns to the Centre of Philosophical Debate

Moran finds it striking that political philosophy developed as a more prominent academic field mainly in the latter part of a century so full of political crises. He discusses the influence of Marxism, including western Marxist movements; Arendt's studies of totalitarianism and human action; and the work of Popper and Isaiah Berlin. John Rawls's A Theory of Justice (1971) brought a major new impetus to political theory.

In France, Sartre and Merleau-Ponty participated in politically engaged debate through Les Temps Modernes, including disagreements over Soviet Communism and anti-colonialism. Moran also identifies Frantz Fanon's The Wretched of the Earth and its discussion of colonial violence and torture. This political history is part of his account of how philosophy responded to—and was transformed by—the century's circumstances.

!Exam Tip

When explaining the post-1945 shift towards the United States, name the historical process, not just "the war": Nazi persecution forced many European intellectuals to leave, often for American institutions. Moran describes a broad change over time, not one event that immediately relocated all philosophical work.

§VIConclusion — The Integrated Legacy

This section answers: How does Moran assess the century's achievements, unresolved questions, and diverse philosophical methods?

Moran identifies substantial technical progress, particularly in logic and discussions of meaning, alongside questions that remained unsettled across the century. He also warns against reducing philosophical history to a "grand narrative" of progress, decline, or permanent conflict between schools. Philosophy became more varied and precise in many areas, but its history still matters: the concepts and problems philosophers use have histories of their own.

The Problem with Grand Narratives and “New Beginnings”

Moran cautions against writing the history of the century as one large, uncomplicated grand narrative—a story claiming that all philosophy moved in a single direction towards success, decline, or one victorious school. Yet the century's thinkers repeatedly announced extraordinary beginnings and endings. Freud and Husserl presented themselves as founders of new fields; Derrida announced the possibility of a science of writing called grammatology, while questioning whether it could be established under inherited metaphysical assumptions.

Heidegger spoke of the “end of philosophy” as the completion of a historical form of metaphysics, not the disappearance of thinking. Foucault treated the modern idea of “man” as a historical invention that might also reach an end. Ayer and logical positivists proposed eliminating metaphysics as literally meaningless according to their criterion of significance. Wittgenstein, especially in his later work, pictured philosophical methods as ways to relieve confusion and bring philosophical questioning to rest. Gadamer, by contrast with Heidegger's destructive questioning, sought to reconnect philosophy with the inherited European tradition.

Philosophy Reflecting on Its Own History

Hegel described philosophy as its time comprehended in thought. Moran takes seriously the task of understanding why a period's philosophical questions and methods developed as they did, while recognizing that history also contains chance, conflict, and unexpected events. The history of philosophy is therefore not merely a catalogue of books and names. Philosophical problems and the concepts used to express them have histories; studying those histories may change how we understand the problems themselves.

Moran also considers why twentieth-century philosophers repeatedly told large stories about their own movements. Lyotard's description of the postmodern condition, Rorty's interpretation of Hegelian historicism, and manifestos for new sciences or new beginnings all attempt to make sense of an era. Moran's point is not that historical events have no intelligible patterns; explanations must also leave room for contingency and for the diversity of philosophical work.

Moran's Final Thought Experiment: “Identity and Discrimination”

Imagine two books with the same title, Identity and Discrimination. One philosopher studies logical and numerical identity: when are two descriptions really about the same thing? How can we tell whether a tree observed at two different times is still the same tree? Here “discrimination” means correctly distinguishing things. A second philosopher examines personal and social identity—gender, family, culture, belonging—and asks whether claims of fixed identity suppress differences between people.

Chapter Summary

Moran asks what remains valuable and what has been left behind in twentieth-century philosophy. The century was exceptionally productive and turbulent: professional philosophy grew within universities, women transformed areas of research, new specialisms appeared, and logic and the study of meaning became more precise. Yet older debates about consciousness, time, knowledge, religion, and moral truth continued. Naturalism grew, but transcendental philosophy remained a powerful response to the challenge of explaining knowledge and normativity.

The century's apparently separate analytic and Continental traditions shared nineteenth-century problems, including the critique of psychologism and speculative idealism. Moran examines the different influence of Nietzsche, Freud, Husserl, Frege, Wittgenstein, and Heidegger, then traces analytic philosophy through Russell, logical positivism, ordinary-language philosophy, Quine, and Davidson. He also studies repeated declarations of philosophical endings, the challenges of writing philosophy's own history, and Europe's changing meaning. World wars, Nazi persecution, forced migration, and later developments in political philosophy profoundly reshaped the discipline. His final example of logical and social identity shows why one word can generate different, potentially connected inquiries. The hope for the twenty-first century is better appreciation of philosophical problems' many-sided character—not the assumption that all differences have already disappeared.

CCentral Thesis

Rowland Stout traces how twentieth-century philosophers examined and refined three major inherited approaches: Aristotle’s character and practical perception, Hume’s emotion and motivation, and Kant’s reason and duty. He argues that the century’s work narrowed some gaps between these traditions, not that every disagreement ended. Utilitarianism remained highly influential despite repeated challenges, while Nietzsche’s criticism of conventional morality offered a contrasting inheritance. Industrialization, totalitarian politics, genocide and the expansion of applied ethics also shaped moral philosophy. Stout approaches the history through five connected questions about moral meaning, moral reality, knowledge, motivation and the structure of ethical systems.

§IThe Conceptual Disruption: Moore's Challenge

This section answers: Why can't "good" simply be defined in terms of something else, like pleasure or desire?

G. E. Moore’s Principia Ethica (1903) challenged earlier philosophers such as J. S. Mill who appeared to define “good” using non-moral terms such as pleasure or desire. The question is whether identifying things that are good also explains what “good” means.

DDefinition — Naturalistic Fallacy

Moore's naturalistic fallacy is the mistake of treating a classification of which things are good (extension) as a definition of what “good” means (intension). He considered goodness simple and non-natural. Stout notes that a similar mistake could occur with a non-naturalistic definition too: extension is not the same as meaning.

DDefinition — The Open Question Argument

Moore's open question argument tests a proposed definition. If “good” simply meant “pleasurable,” asking “This is pleasurable—but is it good?” would be empty, like asking whether a vixen is a female fox. The moral question remains intelligible, so the proposed meaning fails. Stout cautions that this test requires care: we can understand and use words without knowing every part of their meaning.

🍬
Memory Trick — ELI10: The Sugar Test

Suppose someone says a product is good because many people desire it. We can agree it is desired and still ask, “But is it good?” That separates being desired from being desirable. This product is a teaching illustration. Stout's own analogy compares the meaning of “yellow” with the scientific wavelengths reflected by yellow objects.

The Motivational Extension of Moore's Test

Stout explains how later philosophers connected Moore's question with Humean internalism: the view that genuinely judging something good or right involves some motivation to act or feel accordingly. If someone defines “good” as “socially accepted,” we can still ask, “It is socially accepted, but why should I care?” Such a question suggests that approval or conformity does not capture morality's action-guiding force. This further argument depends on accepting internalism; it is not identical to Moore's original argument about meaning.

Quine and the Limits of Moore's Argument

Moore's argument became famous and controversial. Quine's 1951 criticism of the distinction between analytic truths and truths based on facts later raised questions about the very idea of definitions. Yet Stout cautions against assuming that Quine straightforwardly refuted Moore. Moore objected to an analysis of “good”; Quine questioned analytic definitions more generally. Stout also leaves open how precisely Moore's criticism applied to Mill.

Naturalistic Ethics: Grounding Morality Is Not Always Reducing It

Stout distinguishes reducing goodness to a natural property from anchoring ethical ideas in human experience while still allowing moral questions to have their own force. Aristotle's account of virtue and many later naturalists belong to the second project.

Dewey: Moral Principles as Hypotheses

John Dewey argued that enjoyment and human experience supply the material for moral inquiry; they are not themselves finished moral values. We refine our judgment through reflection, imagination and practical testing. Moral rules operate like hypotheses: we try them, see whether they help resolve a situation and revise them if necessary. Stout connects this method to the later language of reflective equilibrium.

Dewey's claim that we construct value has two possible meanings. The weaker interpretation says our interaction determines how we frame a moral problem and which choices we can see. The stronger, anti-realist interpretation says value itself is produced by the rational process. Stout observes that Moore's open question could threaten the stronger claim, but he is not certain that Dewey intended it. Dewey was generally cautious about defining goodness this way.

🧪
An Everyday Picture — Testing a Moral Proposal

A school tries several ways to distribute scarce books. It compares their consequences, hears what students need and adjusts its rules. That is an illustration of Dewey's reflective method, not a source example from Stout. The moral conclusion is not automatically given by everyone's first preference.

Naturalistic and Relativistic Alternatives

Edvard Westermarck explained wrongness using personal or social tendencies to disapprove. R. B. Perry more directly described things as acquiring value when someone takes an interest in them; he later spoke of the harmony of interests. Stout keeps apart the fact that something is valued and the claim that it is valuable.

Roderick Firth's ideal observer is supposed to be omniscient, dispassionate, disinterested and consistent; ethical terms are analyzed through how that observer would react. Richard Brandt instead focuses on attitudes that meet standards acceptable to an intelligent and informed person. Stout distinguishes Brandt's ethical phrase “worthy of approval” from a reduction that defines worthiness through purely non-ethical approval policies.

Gilbert Harman treats moral judgments as relative to a moral framework and its conventions. If an ideal reasoner within framework M condemns an act, Stout asks whether the framework itself is right. Here again, describing an approval is not necessarily the same as justifying it.

Cornell Realism and Moral Natural Kinds

Richard Boyd and Nicholas Sturgeon, associated with Cornell realism, argued that moral properties can be understood as natural kinds. Boyd compares goodness to health: different natural characteristics may form a mutually supporting, homeostatic cluster without being reducible to one short definition. Sturgeon argues that moral properties can help explain why people act or why they judge something bad. Frank Jackson also proposes that “rightness” corresponds to the property that plays its role in mature everyday moral thinking.

Stout does not present this as a settled victory. He asks why a particular natural cluster should matter when deciding what to do. Cornell realists can answer by appealing to critical reflection and the continuity between moral and scientific inquiry, but they still face the task of connecting causal properties with rational moral judgment.

✕Common Mistake — Not All Naturalists Give the Same Definition

Dewey's reflective inquiry, Perry's interests, Firth's ideal observer, Harman's frameworks and Boyd's natural kinds are different projects. Stout's central question is how each connects experience with the meaning, justification and objectivity of morality.

§IINon-Cognitivism: Morality as Expression

This section answers: If moral claims aren't describing facts, what are they doing?

Stout turns to non-cognitivism: an approach that treats a moral judgment not primarily as a description of a fact but as an expression of an attitude, feeling, instruction or norm. That is why the usual categories of truth and falsity do not straightforwardly apply. This approach was shaped by linguistic philosophy, Hume's account of motivation and logical positivism.

DDefinition — Non-Cognitivism

The view that moral statements lack truth-value — they are neither true nor false, because they do not describe facts about the world.

✕Common Mistake — The Geach Problem

Peter Geach's embedding problem asks what a non-cognitivist should say when a moral judgment occurs inside a conditional. Stout's example is “If stealing is wrong then taxation is immoral.” The speaker has not asserted “stealing is wrong” on its own, so simply replacing it with “Stealing—Blast!” makes the conditional hard to understand. The issue is the logical function of moral language inside a larger argument.

How Emotivism Began

In The Meaning of Meaning (1923), C. K. Ogden and I. A. Richards argued that the ethical use of “good” does not refer to a further property of the object. Unlike “red,” which describes something, the word can serve as a sign of approval and may encourage others to respond similarly. A. J. Ayer developed a similar approach in Language, Truth and Logic (1936), and Charles Stevenson emphasized that ethical language can influence listeners even when it includes some descriptive content.

C. D. Broad called this the interjectional theory. On this view, saying that self-sacrifice is good functions much like expressing enthusiasm for self-sacrifice; calling deception bad functions much like expressing disapproval. The point is not that the words are meaningless noises to the speaker, but that their moral function is expressive rather than descriptive.

The Interpersonal Open Question

Stout identifies a difficulty Moore's original test does not capture. Suppose one passenger says to another, “You should not smoke in this compartment.” The second understands the disapproval but can ask, “Why does your disapproval give me a reason not to smoke?” Moral disagreement seems to involve reasons a person might accept, not merely incompatible feelings or instructions. Stout argues that non-cognitivists sought to recover as much objectivity as possible without giving up their basic expressive account. In his assessment, the interpersonal difficulty nevertheless remains.

Hare: Universal Prescriptivism

R. M. Hare argued in The Language of Morals (1952) that moral statements primarily prescribe: “You ought not to smoke here” guides action much like “Do not smoke here!” Unlike an ordinary instruction, however, a moral prescription is universalizable. If I judge an action wrong in one situation, I am committed to the same judgment in every situation with the same morally relevant features.

Stout explains two objections. First, universalizability may be a substantive moral requirement, not merely a rule of grammar. Second, deciding which features are morally relevant already requires moral thought, threatening Hare's explanation with circularity.

DDefinition — Universalizability

In Hare's account, a moral judgment commits the speaker to prescribing the same action in all relevantly similar cases. An ordinary command need not carry this commitment. The difficult question is what counts as a morally relevant similarity.

Blackburn and Gibbard: Keeping Logical Connections

Simon Blackburn's quasi-realism tries to explain “if … then” reasoning as expressing approval of a transition between attitudes: for example, from disapproving of theft to disapproving of an act treated as theft. Allan Gibbard develops a related approach by analyzing judgments about rationality as expressions of accepted norms. These theories seek to explain why moral discourse has the logical shape of statements that could be true or false, even where the basic explanatory theory is expressivist.

§IIIThe Metaphysics of Value: Realism vs. Anti-Realism

This section answers: Do moral facts actually exist "out there" in the world?

Error Theory (John Mackie)

J. L. Mackie argued that ordinary moral talk presents itself as describing objective value, but there are no values of the kind that such talk presupposes. His error theory therefore treats just about every straightforward first-order moral judgment as false. This is different from non-cognitivism: Mackie regards moral judgments as making claims that could be true or false, while denying that their supposed objective truth-makers exist.

Cornell Realism (Boyd/Sturgeon)

Cornell realists defend objective natural moral properties. Boyd associates moral terms with homeostatic clusters of mutually supporting natural features, comparable with health; Sturgeon argues that moral properties can help explain judgments and conduct. This is a realist proposal and remains subject to Stout's question about why a natural cluster supplies a reason for action.

Sensibility Theory (McDowell/Wiggins)

John McDowell compares moral properties with secondary qualities such as color. Something can be red independently of anyone actually looking at it, yet being red is tied to how it would appear in suitable human experience. Likewise, moral judgment may depend on trained human sensibility without being correct merely because one person happens to approve. Stout also discusses David Wiggins, whose qualified, Hume-influenced subjectivism approaches objectivity through standards or “marks of truth.”

Mackie's Two Kinds of “Queerness”

Mackie’s argument in Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong (1977) has two parts. Metaphysically, values would have to be strange entities: objective features of the world that are also inherently action-guiding. Epistemologically, knowing such values would seem to demand a special faculty beyond ordinary perception. Mackie argues that it is more reasonable not to posit them. Stout contrasts this with both natural-kind realism and McDowell’s less metaphysically extravagant account of objectivity.

Nagel: From One's Own Interests to Everyone's

Thomas Nagel does not say that morality requires abandoning the subjective perspective. He seeks to integrate it with a more detached, objective perspective. His Possibility of Altruism (1970) starts with prudence: rational concern for my own future interests. If I accept that my needs are reasons to act because they are needs, rather than merely because they happen to be mine, then other people's needs must also count as reasons. Nagel develops altruism as a requirement of practical reason.

Korsgaard: Practical Identity and Normativity

Christine Korsgaard extends a Kantian approach while bringing it closer to Aristotle. She argues that reflecting on ourselves as agents, and on our practical identity, helps us understand how our humanity provides authority for action. Stout treats this as another attempt to connect rational reflection, agency and moral normativity.

★High-Yield Distinction — What Must Realism Explain?

Stout does not simply divide theories into those that believe in strange external moral objects and those that do not. A theory of moral truth, a theory of moral facts, and a theory of standards independent of individual approval can be related without being identical. The discussion of Wiggins, Mackie, McDowell and Nagel makes these distinctions clear.

§IVEpistemology: Intuition and the "Moral Gaze"

This section answers: How do we justify a moral judgment — through rules, intuition, or perception?

Intuitionism (W.D. Ross)

W. D. Ross rejected the idea that one single principle, such as maximizing good consequences, settles every question of duty. He identified seven types of prima facie duty: fidelity, reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, self-improvement and non-maleficence. Some look to consequences, while others depend on our past actions or relationships. They can conflict, and Ross argues that practical judgment must determine the actual duty in a particular case; Stout emphasizes that these decisions are often more or less probable, not infallible.

DDefinition — Prima Facie Duty

Ross's prima facie duty is a morally relevant consideration that counts in favor of an action unless competing duties outweigh it. This is not a superficial first impression: such a duty gives a genuine reason, even when it is not the decisive actual duty after all considerations are weighed.

Attention and Vision (Iris Murdoch)

Iris Murdoch, influenced by Simone Weil, emphasizes attention: the difficult ethical work of seeing another person and their situation accurately rather than viewing them through our own wishes or self-importance. The improvement of our perception and emotional response matters to morality before we even reach a moment of choice. Stout relates this to the broader Aristotelian sensibility tradition.

Sidgwick's Three Forms of Intuitionism

Intuitionism claims that some moral knowledge is non-inferential: it is not established merely by deriving it from a prior principle. Stout begins with Henry Sidgwick, whose Methods of Ethics (1874) described three forms. Perceptional intuitionism judges individual situations directly; dogmatic intuitionism relies on general rules without deeper arguments; and philosophical intuitionism seeks fundamental principles at which the chain of justification stops. Sidgwick thought the first two stages inadequate and ended with fundamental intuitions, including the maximization of good.

Moore's Beautiful and Ugly Worlds

Moore rejected Sidgwick's idea that happiness alone is intrinsically good. Stout recalls Moore's example of two worlds without people or other conscious beings: one beautiful, the other ugly. Moore thought there would still be a reason to favor the beautiful world. Yet Moore continued to agree with Sidgwick that the right act is the one that produces the greatest good. This opened the way for Ross to ask whether maximizing good can be wrong when it violates justice or an obligation.

Prichard, Broad and Ross: Why Duties May Look Backward

H. A. Prichard and C. D. Broad helped develop accounts of duty not reducible to consequences. Broad spoke of features that make an action fitting or unfitting. Ross argued that we often must consider what we have already done—for example, a promise made, an injury to repair or help already received—not only the good we might produce in the future. An obligation of fidelity is not simply another calculation of total happiness.

Stout describes the approach as robust in applied practice even though it does not supply one mechanical formula to resolve every conflict.

Why Intuition Need Not Mean a Mysterious Power

Stout uses the example of a tree surgeon. An experienced tree surgeon may recognize an unhealthy tree without first deducing the diagnosis from a general rule. This can be a trained recognitional skill, involving observation and understanding. Likewise, moral intuition can depend on refined capacities to see what matters in a situation; it does not automatically require a strange non-natural faculty. Non-inferential is not the same as irrational.

Particularism and the Education of Moral Sensibility

Ross still allowed general prima facie duties to provide some reasons for action. Later particularists such as John McDowell and Jonathan Dancy questioned whether moral judgments need even this support from universal principles. A consideration can count differently in different situations; recognizing its significance demands attention to the particular case, not merely the application of a fixed rule.

Stout connects this with Aristotle's account of moral sensibility. Reason, perception and properly educated emotions contribute together to moral judgment. G. H. von Wright describes practical reasoning through wanting an end, recognizing what is needed and setting oneself to act. Martha Nussbaum stresses the connection between the agent's desires and how the world appears to them. David Wiggins recalls Aristotle's aesthesis—situational appreciation. The ethical vocabulary involved may be irreducible to a purely detached, emotionless description, without thereby being immune to justification.

DIntuition and Sensibility Are Not Opposites

Intuition here is non-inferential moral recognition. Sensibility is the cultivated ability to perceive and respond appropriately, integrating reason and emotion. Stout's discussion shows how later Aristotelian approaches developed the earlier intuitionist tradition rather than merely rejecting it.

Virtue, Care, and the Particular Person

Elizabeth Anscombe argued that modern talk of moral obligation often presupposed a lawgiver while detaching duties from that framework. She called for better moral psychology and renewed attention to virtue. Philippa Foot and Alasdair MacIntyre developed influential virtue-centered approaches. Stout also discusses Bernard Williams's concern for personal integrity, and his claim that reasons for action must connect with the agent's motivational set. This challenges moral theories that treat a person merely as a location where impersonal outcomes are produced.

Carol Gilligan contrasted an approach that emphasizes abstract justice with an ethics of care attentive to relationships, context and the preservation of human connections. Max Scheler considered emotion's role in apprehending value; Martin Buber and Emmanuel Levinas developed approaches concerned with encounters between persons. Stout places these currents within the wider movement from single abstract principles towards the character, perception and relations of the agent.

§VMoral Psychology: Agency and Responsibility

This section answers: What has to be true of an agent for them to be morally responsible?

After discussing how we judge what is good or right, Stout turns to agency: what makes a movement an action, and what makes an agent morally responsible for it? These are connected but distinct questions. The century's debate drew on Hume's account of beliefs and desires, Kant's concern with practical reason, and later work on freedom and interpersonal responsibility.

Strawson's move is often mis-described as "solving" free will. It doesn't — it sidesteps the metaphysical debate by relocating responsibility into our interpersonal "reactive attitudes." Stout emphasizes that Strawson shifts the discussion from the “objective” standpoint of a detached observer to our participant attitudes towards other people. Crucially, these attitudes are not simply whatever we happen to feel: our resentment or gratitude can be moderated by reasons and circumstances.

Davidson: Reasons Can Explain and Cause Actions

Humean approaches connect action with an agent's beliefs and desires. Kantian approaches give a special role to practical reason. In “Actions, Reasons, and Causes” (1963), Donald Davidson combined these themes: a primary reason can make an action rationally intelligible and also be part of its causal explanation. He challenged non-causal accounts associated with Ryle, Anscombe and Melden.

Two Challenges to Moral Responsibility

Causal origination: Paul Edwards and Galen Strawson argued that if a person is to be responsible for an action, they must also be responsible for its causes. Yet the causal chain may reach back beyond anything that person controls. Alternative possibilities: another principle says responsibility requires that the person could have acted otherwise. Peter van Inwagen used this second principle to argue that freedom and determinism are incompatible.

These concerns arise against a scientific picture in which events are either law-governed or probabilistic. Hard determinists regard this as threatening responsibility; compatibilists hold that free action can coexist with determinism; and libertarians reject determinism where they think it would eliminate free will. Richard Taylor and Roderick Chisholm developed versions of agent causation: the agent herself, not merely prior events or states, originates an action. Stout notes its substantial metaphysical cost and later defenses such as Tim O'Connor's.

Daniel Dennett argued that “could have done otherwise” can mean different things depending on what is held fixed. In discussing possible actions, we must not confuse a rigid physical description of one exact event with the ordinary capacities we attribute to agents.

Frankfurt's Counterexample: No Alternative, Yet Responsible

Harry Frankfurt (1969) imagines someone who freely decides to carry out a bad act. Unknown to the agent, an observer is ready to intervene and force that very decision if the agent begins to choose differently. But the observer never intervenes, because the agent chooses the act independently. Frankfurt argues that the agent is still responsible despite lacking an available alternative. His example challenges the necessity of alternative possibilities; it does not by itself settle every question about determinism.

🛤️
The Unused Intervener — Keep the Two Paths Separate

What actually happens: the agent decides for herself and acts. What would have happened otherwise: an observer was ready to force the same outcome. The second path never occurs. The example asks why we judge the actual choice responsible even when there was no way for the final outcome to differ.

P. F. Strawson and Reactive Attitudes

In “Freedom and Resentment” (1962), P. F. Strawson argues that responsibility is part of the relationships we live through. We feel resentment when treated without respect and gratitude when treated well. We can also respond on behalf of others, then reflect on when such responses are appropriate. Stout therefore presents responsibility as connected with a rationally educated reactive sensibility, not merely with unexamined emotions. Seeing someone only as an object of scientific explanation leaves out this participant perspective.

Self-Definition: Sartre, Beauvoir and Charles Taylor

Existentialists such as Sartre understand self-definition as a radical choice that creates value rather than following a previously established value. Simone de Beauvoir identifies a tension: every free decision gives meaning to a life, but in giving that meaning it also limits the freedom to redefine it. She calls this ambiguity.

Charles Taylor offers a more explicitly rational account. When someone faces conflicting evaluations, they can reconceive the situation and redefine themselves in a way that absorbs earlier tensions. Unlike the radical existentialist leap as Stout describes it, Taylor's self-definition remains open to assessment and revision. The disagreement concerns the relationship between freedom, rationality and the creation of values.

DDefinition — The Veil of Ignorance

John Rawls's veil of ignorance asks people to choose principles for a society without knowing what place they themselves will occupy in it. Stout presents this as a way to determine which institutional arrangements could reasonably be accepted.

T. M. Scanlon argued that an act is wrong when it would be forbidden by a system of rules no one could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced agreement. This tests the justifiability of rules to persons, not simply whether those rules produce the largest sum of satisfaction.

Think of the Veil of Ignorance as designing the rules of a game before you know which player-piece you'll be handed. You'd design fair rules, because you might end up as the weakest piece on the board.

What Does Utilitarianism Aim to Maximize?

Consequentialism judges an action by the goodness or badness of its outcomes. Utilitarianism is the major consequentialist view discussed by Stout, but philosophers disagreed about what counts as a good outcome. Hedonistic versions focus on pleasure or other satisfactory mental states. Ideal utilitarianism, associated with Moore and others, makes room for such goods as beauty, knowledge, human flourishing and relationships. Preference utilitarianism asks how fully people's preferences are satisfied.

Nozick's Experience Machine

Robert Nozick asks whether someone would enter a machine that could provide all the experiences of achieving a cherished goal—say, writing a great book—without actually achieving it. If the person would refuse, they must care about doing and achieving real things, not only about pleasant feelings. Stout presents the thought experiment as a challenge to theories that identify all value with an inner mental state.

Ramsey, Preferences, and Rational Choice

Frank Ramsey (1931) developed ways of measuring a person's preferences and beliefs through choices and bets. Von Neumann and Morgenstern developed game theory, while John Harsanyi developed preference utilitarianism. These methods had enormous practical influence on welfare economics and cost–benefit analysis.

Stout also presents an objection. The fact that an agent does what they prefer does not show that rationality consists only in satisfying pre-existing preferences. Preferences themselves can be questioned through ethical deliberation. The shift from what is desired to what is desirable echoes the earlier debates around Mill and Perry.

Who Counts in the Calculation?

Peter Singer argues in Animal Liberation (1975) that all and only sentient beings—those capable of experiencing pleasure or pain—should count in the calculation of overall happiness. Stout notes how this leads Singer to controversial positions on animals and other practical issues. Derek Parfit, in Reasons and Persons (1984), asks us to take the interests of future generations seriously. This extends moral questions into the environment, reproduction and the identity of people not yet born.

Stout's Utilitarian Judge: Release the Murderer?

Stout's specific example is a judge who knows that the defendant is a murderer but also knows that following normal judicial procedure will lead to riots and great loss of life. If releasing the murderer prevents the riots, an act-utilitarian calculation tells the judge to release him, even though justice and the law point the other way. The problem is not whether a legal system can be justified by its useful consequences, but whether a judge must follow its rules in a particular case when disobedience would produce more happiness.

★Source-Fidelity Point — The Judge Scenario

Stout's actual scenario: release a known murderer to avoid riots. Some study guides discuss a different hypothetical in which a judge frames an innocent person. That is not Stout's example. Here we retain the source's causal structure and the question it raises about following the law.

Three Attempts to Justify Rules

Rules of thumb: A utilitarian may follow rules because recalculating every outcome is difficult. Stout argues this will not help when breaking a rule plainly produces better consequences. Motive-utilitarianism: Robert Adams investigates the value of cultivating good character and motives, including a strong aversion to injustice. Hare's two levels: everyday intuitive thinking follows established principles and dispositions, but critical utilitarian thinking can override them in exceptional cases. Stout points out that Hare's judge may still have to choose the legally unjust course.

Rule-utilitarianism, introduced by R. F. Harrod and analyzed by David Lyons, combines utility with a generalization principle: act in line with the rules whose general acceptance would maximize utility. J. J. C. Smart criticized this approach for tending to collapse into act-utilitarianism; Stout adds that Lyons showed some formulations avoid collapse, though they are not always plausible for that reason.

⚖️
A Simple Contrast — Act and Rule

Act question: What will bring the most good now? Rule question: Which rules would bring the most good if generally accepted? Stout then asks whether the second question truly gives rules the authority they need when breaking a rule would seem better in one case.

Self-Interest, Commitment, and the Prisoners' Dilemma

Some philosophers tried to justify morality by showing that principled cooperation serves everyone's interests. Stephen Toulmin emphasized a harmony of interests; Kurt Baier argued for moral reasons that override simple self-interest while still being connected with what serves our interests. The prisoners' dilemma is a problem for this project: if everyone pursues an immediate advantage by cheating, all can end up worse off than they would under cooperation.

David Gauthier argues that rationality involves the ability to commit ourselves to principles, allowing cooperation to survive temptations to defect. Alan Gewirth asks why it would be reasonable to demand respect for one's own generic rights without recognizing similar rights in others. Both try to ground moral constraints without simply relying on one person's immediate advantage.

Rights: Thomson and Raz

Judith Jarvis Thomson, developing a problem also discussed by Philippa Foot, asks why it can be acceptable to divert a trolley so that one person dies instead of five, yet unacceptable to kill a healthy patient for organs that would save five. The difference cannot be explained merely by counting lives saved; it invites questions about the rights of the people affected. Stout also mentions Thomson's influential abortion argument, which appeals to a mother's right to decide who may use her body.

Joseph Raz links rights to interests: when an aspect of someone's well-being is a sufficient reason to put another person under a duty, the first person has a right. Rights are not simply assumed as morally basic; they are explained through interests, duties and flourishing. Stout contrasts this with approaches that treat rights as fundamental.

Contractualism: Scanlon, Rawls and Habermas

Contractualism uses actual or hypothetical agreements to examine what principles people could reasonably accept. T. M. Scanlon asks which rules for conduct no person could reasonably reject as a basis for informed, unforced general agreement. John Rawls imagines choosing social principles behind a veil of ignorance, without knowing one's future position in society. Jürgen Habermas emphasizes properly inclusive discussion where all participants must respect one another's perspectives.

Stout treats these as important neo-Kantian developments and explicitly notes that the discussion now begins to pass from moral philosophy into political philosophy. He does not reduce Scanlon's individual morality, Rawls's institutional justice and Habermas's discourse theory to one identical system.

This section answers: What was the overall trajectory of the century's moral philosophy?

Stout describes increased sophistication and some narrowing of differences among Aristotelian, Humean and Kantian approaches, not a completed reconciliation into one doctrine. Philosophers refined accounts of moral language, truth, knowledge, feeling, action, responsibility, consequences and principles. Chapter Summary

Rowland Stout organizes twentieth-century moral philosophy around five overlapping questions: conceptual (what does moral language mean?), metaphysical (are there moral facts?), epistemological (how can we know or justify values?), moral psychological (how are judgments, motives and responsibility related?) and normative (what rules or principles should guide action?). He describes increasingly sophisticated work and some convergence among Aristotelian, Humean and Kantian traditions, while preserving their real disagreements. Moore challenges definitions of goodness; Dewey, other naturalists and Cornell realists explore the relation between natural experience and value; non-cognitivists reinterpret ethical language, meeting objections from the interpersonal open question and Geach's embedding problem. Mackie argues against objective values; Wiggins, McDowell, Nagel and Korsgaard offer different ways to understand moral truth, objectivity and reason. Sidgwick and Ross develop intuitionist theories, later connected with particularism, sensibility and virtue. Davidson, Frankfurt, Strawson, Sartre, Beauvoir and Taylor deepen questions about agency and freedom. Finally, utilitarians reconsider happiness, preferences, rules and moral scope, while rights theorists and contractualists examine justice and legitimate obligations. Stout's narrative moves into political philosophy rather than declaring that every ethical dispute has been settled.

CCentral Thesis

Philosophy of science is the systematic investigation into the validity of human knowledge and the nature of the reality science describes. It bifurcates into two inquiries: Epistemology (should we believe scientific claims, given the evidence?) and Metaphysics (what must the world be like, if those claims are true?). The field's central tension is the Problem of Induction — the gap between finite past observations and universal future laws — compounded by the challenge of Unobservability, where science posits entities (quarks, viruses) beyond direct human senses.

§IThe Epistemological Gateway: Induction and its Rivals

This section answers: What justifies believing a scientific law will keep holding tomorrow?

1. The Problem of Induction

Science seeks universal laws (e.g. "all sodium heated on a Bunsen burner glows orange"). But these laws are derived via Induction: moving from a finite number of past observations to a general conclusion about how nature will always behave.

  • The Logical Gap: unlike Deduction (premises guarantee the conclusion), induction offers no guarantee — there is no logical necessity that the future will resemble the past.
🦢
Memory Trick — ELI10

Imagine you've only ever seen white swans. You conclude "all swans are white." That's induction. The "Problem" is that the very next swan could be black, and your "law" would be shattered.

2. Popper's Falsificationism

Sir Karl Popper argued science does not actually use induction. Instead it uses Conjectures and Refutations: scientists propose a theory (conjecture) and then try to prove it wrong (falsification). A theory that survives is "undefeated," but never "proven."

DDefinition — The Demarcation Problem

Popper used falsifiability to distinguish science from pseudo-science (Marxism, Psychoanalysis, Astrology). Science makes precise predictions that could be wrong (Newtonian physics); pseudo-science is so vague that any outcome can be explained away (Astrology).

✕Common Mistake — Popper's Failing

Don't present Falsificationism as a flawless solution. Popper's theory struggles to explain "Positive Knowledge." We build bridges and cure diseases because we believe certain theories are right — not merely because they haven't yet been proven wrong. A purely falsificationist picture cannot easily account for this practical confidence.

3. Bayesianism (Degree of Belief)

Bayesians argue science is about subjective probability. Rationality means updating your "degree of belief" in a hypothesis as new evidence (E) arrives.

DBayes's Theorem

Prob(H/E) = Prob(H) × Prob(E/H) / Prob(E)

If evidence is surprising but predicted by your theory, your belief in that theory should increase significantly.

🎰
Memory Trick — The Dutch Book Argument

If your degrees of belief don't follow the rules of probability, a clever gambler could construct a set of bets against you such that you lose money regardless of the outcome. This is the classic justification for why rational belief should obey probability theory.

§IIThe Battle for Reality: Realism vs. Instrumentalism

This section answers: When science talks about electrons, is it describing something real, or just giving us a useful calculating tool?

1. The Realist Stance

Realists believe science provides an accurate description of the unobservable world (atoms, electrons).

★High-Yield Fact — The "No Miracles" Argument

Science lets us send people to the moon and predict novel phenomena. This would be a "miracle" if the underlying theories weren't at least approximately true — this is the classic Argument from Success for scientific realism.

2. The Instrumentalist Challenge

Instrumentalists view theories as "calculating tools" rather than literal truths.

  • Underdetermination of Theory (UTD): based on the Duhem–Quine Thesis — for any set of data, multiple incompatible theories could fit it. We can always "save" a theory by changing auxiliary hypotheses.
  • Pessimistic Meta-induction: history shows most past theories (Phlogiston, Indivisible Atoms) were eventually proven false. Therefore our current theories are probably false too.

3. Theory-Dependence and Incommensurability

Kuhn and Feyerabend argued that even "seeing" is theoretical.

👁️
Clarifying Example — The Müller–Lyer Illusion

Two lines of identical length appear different because of arrow-shaped fins at their ends. This is a visual proof that our sensory systems already use "theories" about the world to interpret raw data — undermining the idea of a neutral, theory-free observation.

DDefinition — Incommensurability

There is no neutral "yardstick" to compare two scientific theories/paradigms, because the very language of observation changes when the theory changes.

This section answers: What, metaphysically, makes something a cause, a law, or a probability — rather than just a coincidence?

1. Causation and Explanation

Modern metaphysics of causation began with David Hume's Constant Conjunction: we never actually see a "cause," only one event consistently following another.

2. Laws vs. Accidents

Not all regularities are laws. "All the screws in my desk are copper" is a true regularity, but not a law of nature.

  • Systematization (Ramsey–Lewis): laws are the axioms of the simplest, most powerful deductive system of our knowledge.
  • Necessitation (Armstrong): laws are a "natural necessity" — a metaphysical link between properties that goes beyond mere coincidence.

§IVQuantum Mechanics and Biological Purpose

This section answers: Do the special sciences (quantum physics, biology) break the tidy epistemic/metaphysical picture built above?
DDefinition — The Measurement Problem

On the orthodox view of Quantum Mechanics, the "wave function" (a spread of probabilities) only collapses into a determinate reality when a measurement is made. This leads to the famous Schrödinger's Cat paradox: is the cat both dead and alive until we look?

Teleology in Biology

We explain the polar bear's fur by its future purpose (camouflage). Modern philosophers see this as a "disguised" causal explanation: past survival advantage (natural selection) caused the trait to persist — the "purpose" language is shorthand for a causal history, not literal forward-looking design.

Reductionism

The idea that biology reduces to chemistry, and chemistry to physics.

DDefinition — Token-Identity

Every particular biological event is a physical event, but there may be no general physical law that covers all instances of, say, "aggressive behavior" across every different species.

!Exam Tip

If asked to evaluate teleological explanation in biology, don't simply say "it's outdated." The stronger answer notes that teleological language survives in modern biology as a convenient shorthand for a genuinely causal, backward-looking selection story — it has been reinterpreted, not eliminated.

§VConclusion

The investigation of science moves from the Epistemological (justification of induction; realism vs. instrumentalism) to the Metaphysical (causation, laws, probability). Science is not a simple collection of facts but a complex structure of conjectures navigating the limits of observation and the logical gaps of induction. Whether science describes a literal reality (Realism) or acts as a highly effective map (Instrumentalism), it remains the most successful tool for navigating the natural world.

"A single contrary example suffices for a conclusive disproof, but no number of supporting examples will constitute a conclusive proof."

1.1 · 20th-Century PhilosophySource: Blackwell Companion↑ Contents

Sartre, Foucault and Derrida

From radical freedom, to power/knowledge, to the instability of language

In This Chapter — Three Movements

  1. The Sovereignty of the Subject (Sartre) — Being-for-itself vs. Being-in-itself; freedom as negation.
  2. The Archeology of Power (Foucault) — from the mind to the "episteme"; madness, criminality, and surveillance.
  3. The Instability of Language (Derrida) — dismantling binary oppositions; différance; the impossibility of pure presence.
"Nothingness must, therefore, exist like 'a worm in the heart of being'." — Jean-Paul Sartre
CCentral Thesis

Late twentieth-century French philosophy pivots from Existential Phenomenology, which grounds the world in human consciousness and radical freedom, to Poststructuralism, which de-centres the human subject in favour of the underlying structures of history, power, and language. While Sartre viewed the individual as the absolute source of meaning, Foucault and Derrida challenged this "subjective" dominance: Foucault revealed identity as a contingent product of historical power regimes; Derrida showed that Western thought's own logic is built on unstable linguistic oppositions that perpetually "deconstruct" themselves.

§IJean-Paul Sartre: The Philosopher of Radical Freedom

This section answers: If consciousness has no fixed nature, what makes us free — and why do most people flee from that freedom?

Sartre's philosophy, primarily detailed in Being and Nothingness, is built on the nature of consciousness and its relationship to the world.

1. The Nature of Consciousness (Being-for-itself)

Sartre adopts Husserl's concept of Intentionality: consciousness is always of something. But Sartre adds a radical twist: consciousness itself is nothing.

  • Transparency: consciousness has no "inside," no soul, no spiritual substance — its entire existence is exhausted by its relation to the objects it perceives.
  • Being-for-itself: unlike a rock, consciousness is self-aware; this internal relation to itself makes it "for-itself."
  • Being-in-itself: the world of objects — "brute, unstructured givens" that are "superfluous" and "absurd," with no intrinsic meaning until consciousness interprets them.

2. Nihilation and the "Worm in Being"

How does meaning enter the world? Through Nihilation (néantisation).

☕
Clarifying Example — The Café

If you are looking for Pierre in a café and he is not there, you do not simply see a room — you see a "not-Pierre" café. You have introduced nothingness into the scene, just by expecting and failing to find him.

DDefinition — Freedom as Withdrawal

Sartre identifies the ability to say "no" or "not" with Freedom. Freedom is the "withdrawal of consciousness from being." Because we are not "things" (Being-in-itself), we are not bound by causal laws. We are, in his phrase, "condemned to be free."

3. Bad Faith and Authenticity

Most humans flee from the "anguish" of absolute freedom.

  • Bad Faith (Mauvaise foi): lying to oneself by pretending one is a "thing" with a fixed nature — e.g. "I can't help it, that's just my personality."
  • Authenticity: the ethical goal — accepting one's status as radically free, and recognising oneself as the "ultimate source of all meaning."

§IIMichel Foucault: The Historian of Systems

This section answers: If Sartre says the individual creates meaning, what happens when we discover the "rules of the game" were already there before we were born?

Foucault represents the "poststructuralist reaction." He rejects Sartre's focus on the individual subject, looking instead at the "objective structures" that shape human experience.

1. Archeology and the Episteme

Foucault uses Archeology to uncover the "epistemic unconscious" — the hidden rules of a time period that dictate what can be said and what counts as truth. In The Order of Things, he argues disciplines like biology or linguistics are not "progressing" toward truth, but merely shifting from one epistemic structure to another.

2. The History of Madness

Foucault identifies two major "breaks" in how Europe viewed madness:

Renaissance
Madness was an alternative mode of human existence — a different kind of "reason"
Classical Age (17th c.)
Madness redefined as "Unreason"; the mad were physically excluded (confinement), seen as having "plunged into animality"
Modern Age (19th c.+)
The mad are brought back into society but treated as "mental illness"; the asylum was not a humanitarian gift but a "gigantic moral imprisonment" designed to control the mad through guilt

3. Power, Knowledge, and the Body

In Discipline and Punish, Foucault introduces Genealogy (influenced by Nietzsche).

DDefinition — Power/Knowledge

Knowledge is not a "pure" pursuit; it is inextricably tied to power. Power generates knowledge about the people it wants to control, and that knowledge, in turn, increases power.

  • Surveillance: modern society moves from the "spectacle" of public execution to a "society of surveillance" — control exercised by monitoring bodies in schools, factories, prisons.
  • The Self-Scrutinizing Subject: in History of Sexuality, Foucault notes we are now controlled by our own knowledge of ourselves. Trying to find our "true" sexual nature, we merely conform to norms laid down by scientific disciplines.
🎲
Memory Trick — ELI10

Imagine you are playing a game. Sartre says you are the one who decides the rules of the game. Foucault says the game board, the pieces, and the rules were already there before you were born, and they actually decide who you are allowed to be.

!Exam Tip

When explaining Power/Knowledge, resist reducing it to "knowledge is used by the powerful." Foucault's stronger and more examinable claim is that power and knowledge are mutually constitutive — power doesn't just use pre-existing knowledge, it actively produces the categories (madness, sexuality, criminality) that knowledge then studies.

§IIIJacques Derrida: The Reader of Textual Limits

This section answers: If Foucault shows the mind is shaped by external structures, what happens when we discover that language — the very tool we think with — cannot deliver the fixed truths philosophy has always wanted?

Derrida constantly returns to the "body of failed philosophical knowledge" to dissect it.

1. Logocentrism and Deconstruction

Western philosophy is Logocentric: obsessed with finding a "pure presence" or absolute truth (God, The Good, The Self). This rests on Binary Oppositions:

  • Presence vs. Absence
  • Truth vs. Falsity
  • Speech vs. Writing (Speech usually prioritized as "purer")
DDefinition — Deconstruction

The technique of showing that binary oppositions are unstable. The "lower" term (e.g. writing) is often actually necessary for the "higher" term (e.g. speech) to exist at all.

2. Différance: Difference and Deferral

Derrida coined the neologism différance (with an "a") to describe how meaning works. It carries two meanings simultaneously:

  • To Differ: a sign (word) only has meaning because it differs from other signs.
  • To Defer: meaning is never fully "present" right now — it is always put off until the next sign, and the next.
★High-Yield Fact — Pattern Recognition

The "a" in différance is visible in writing but silent in speech. This subverts the very "priority of speech" that Logocentrism assumes — because writing can express a distinction that speech cannot. The pun is the argument.

3. Ethics and Justice

For Derrida, Justice is not the same as Law.

  • Law: a system of rules that is stable and can be deconstructed.
  • Justice: an infinite demand that can never be fully satisfied by any law.

Deciding what is just in a specific moment requires a "leap" or a "madness," because the law can never fully tell you what is right in a unique situation.

§IVConclusion — From the Internal to the External

The journey from Sartre to Derrida moves from the Internal to the External. Sartre finds the "nothingness" inside the human mind that allows for absolute freedom. Foucault finds that this "mind" is actually constructed by external historical "epistemes" and power structures. Derrida finalises this by showing that even our language — the tool we use to think — is a system of shifting differences that can never capture absolute truth.

Continental Philosophy · The Meaning Crisis
1.1 · 20th-Century PhilosophySource: Continental Philosophy↑ Contents

The Gap Between Knowledge and Wisdom

Why more facts have not brought more meaning

In This Chapter — The Historical & Conceptual "Split"

  1. The Ancient Foundation — a unified circle where knowledge and wisdom were one.
  2. The 17th-Century Fracture — modern science demands empirical proof; philosophy becomes an "under-labourer."
  3. The Modern Paradox — as knowledge increases, the "felt gap" of meaning grows more acute.
  4. The Continental Bridge — thinkers who try to reconnect rigorous truth with the "drama of life."
"The eternal silence of infinite spaces fills me with dread." — Blaise Pascal
CCentral Thesis

The defining crisis of modern thought is the widening "meaning gap" between Knowledge (scientia) — the empirical explanation of how the world functions — and Wisdom — the reflective understanding of what it means to lead a good human life. Ancient philosophy integrated these two realms; the post-17th-century scientific revolution relegated wisdom to the fringes, leaving professional philosophy as a mere "under-labourer" to science. Continental philosophy emerges as the primary attempt to bridge this abyss, insisting that philosophy, to remain valid, must tackle the existential question of the meaning of life.

§IThe Ancient Ideal: Philosophy as Formation

This section answers: What did philosophy look like before knowledge and wisdom were pulled apart?

In the ancient world, philosophy was defined as the "love of wisdom." It was not merely an academic exercise but a reflective practice aimed at "forming" human beings rather than just "informing" them.

  • The Examined Life: for Socrates and his successors, "the unexamined life is not worth living." The reverse is also true: "the unlived life is not worth examining" — ancient philosophy was inseparable from the polis, the public realm of political and social life.
  • Teleological Universe: the ancients held a "teleological view," where the cosmos was believed to express human purpose.
DDefinition — Final Cause (Aristotle)

The idea that every natural thing has a goal or purpose for its existence. In this view, nature is a "living book" where theory (understanding) and practice (living) are harmonised.

§IIThe Modern Shift: From Wisdom to Scientia

This section answers: What did the Scientific Revolution actually do to philosophy's job description?

Since the 17th century, philosophy has undergone a radical transformation, moving from a practical pursuit of the "good life" to a theoretical inquiry into the conditions of knowledge.

  • The Rise of Epistemology: modern philosophy is dominated by the question "How do we know what we know?" — it becomes the "theory of knowledge."
DDefinition — The "Under-Labourer" Status

John Locke famously described the philosopher's role not as a leader, but as a "janitor" in the "Crystal Palace of the sciences" — tasked with clearing away the rubbish that blocks scientific progress.

  • Knowledge (Scientia): claims must be verified by experiment (e.g. the chemical makeup of water) or they are rejected.
  • Faith vs. Fact: statements about meaning or divinity (e.g. "Jesus is the redeemer") are relegated to faith because they lack empirical proof.

§IIIThe Paradox of Progress and the "Meaning Gap"

This section answers: Why does more scientific progress seem to produce more, not less, existential anxiety?

A "massive paradox": as society becomes more technologically advanced and scientifically "enlightened," the sense of meaninglessness actually increases.

  • Disenchantment of Nature (Max Weber's term): nature is no longer a "world soul" but "sheer, impersonal objective 'stuff'" governed by indifferent physical laws.
  • The Appendix of Wisdom: wisdom is now treated like a vestigial organ — an "appendix we believed we no longer needed," yet it continues to "irritate" us.
  • Luxury and Neurosis: borrowing from Bertolt Brecht ("food first, then ethics"), when humans are "showered with every earthly blessing," they concoct new miseries. The question "What is the point of my life?" returns with a "frightening vengeance."

§IVThe Two Risks: Scientism vs. Obscurantism

This section answers: What happens when people try to fill the meaning gap the wrong way?

When philosophy fails to address the meaning gap, humans fall into one of two dangerous traps, as described by John Stuart Mill.

✕Common Mistake

Don't treat Scientism and Obscurantism as opposites in the sense of "good vs. bad." Both are presented as equally dangerous failure modes — one collapses wisdom into science, the other abandons rigour altogether. The Continental response (§V) is not a midpoint compromise between them, but a genuinely different move: taking the meaning-question seriously without either surrendering to pseudo-science or abandoning intellectual rigour.

The document traces a tragic trajectory: from the Ancient Unity (understanding the world meant understanding how to live), through the Scientific Divorce (nature became a cold, mechanical object), to the Modern Abyss (infinite facts, no meaning). Chapter Summary

Ancient philosophy fused knowledge and wisdom into a single teleological worldview, where understanding nature's purposes and understanding how to live were the same project. The 17th-century Scientific Revolution severed this unity: philosophy was demoted to Locke's "under-labourer," clearing conceptual rubbish for science, while questions of meaning were exiled to faith. The resulting paradox is that scientific and material progress has not closed the "meaning gap" but widened it — Weber's "disenchantment" strips nature of soul, and comfort itself seems to provoke new existential neuroses. Mill's warning identifies two equally dangerous escape routes — Scientism (reducing humans to beasts) and Obscurantism (reducing humans to lunatics) — while Continental philosophy, from Hegel through Camus, offers the only sustained attempt to take the question of meaning seriously without collapsing into either extreme.
