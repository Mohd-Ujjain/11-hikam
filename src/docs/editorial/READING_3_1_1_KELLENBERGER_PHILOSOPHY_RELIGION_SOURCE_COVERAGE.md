## Governing source-specific argument

Kellenberger introduces philosophy of religion not as an isolated theology or an inquiry for believers alone, but as a set of **human** questions emerging from awe, mistaken knowledge, death and tension between scientific determinism and inwardly experienced freedom. In his stipulative opening definitions, the agnostic withholds belief both in God and in the nonexistence of God because human knowledge is insufficient; the atheist **unequivocally believes there is no God**. The sources of religious questions and the demand for reasonable judgment are common even to readers who have left or rejected their religion.

The author explains why **both believers and nonbelievers** must examine the reasonableness of religious views. The believer who considers religion unreasonable but accepts it must arbitrarily compartmentalize faith, while the nonbeliever who rejects religion *on the ground of unreasonableness* must justify that judgment. Arguments intended to prove God exist raise **two independent questions**: do they actually prove God exists (Ch. 2), and, even if so, are they **relevant to religious belief** (Ch. 3)? Chapters 4 and 5 consider non-proof ways of making religion reasonable and the possibility of an experiential basis.

This shared concern extends to the Religious Problem of Evil for an all-good, all-powerful God; the independence of reasons for miracle claims and denials; morality even outside religious life; literal or metaphorical descriptions of God and divine Father/Mother imagery; **religious realism versus nonrealism** on whether spirituality needs an independently existing transcendent God; and plural religions confronting one another in an interconnected society. Nonrealism's significance for a nonbeliever is explicitly **conditional**: if religious spirituality does not need transcendent realism, rejection solely on that ground does not settle the question of religion.

**The concluding methodological claim is central, not an appendix:** Kellenberger combines philosophical clarity and balance with `religious sensibilities`, a sense of religious appropriateness formed by a tradition or a current within it. These sensibilities **illuminate**, introduce neglected questions, and anchor philosophy in the living tradition; they do **not** settle arguments. Different sensibilities within a tradition and reactions *against* religion also contribute. Philosophy thereby speaks to religion, and religion speaks to philosophy.

## Specific errors corrected from previous 3.1.1

1. Old title and source field omitted **James Kellenberger**; fixed in frontmatter and TOC.
2. Previous “agnostic means humans *cannot* know” was too narrow; source says they **do not or cannot know**, and the double suspension of belief is essential.
3. Previous use of `Validity` for the first proof test risks confusing a logically valid inference with an actually successful demonstration. The source asks whether the argument **in fact proves** God exists, *and independently* whether it is religiously relevant.
4. The earlier dossier had inadequate treatment of Chapters 4–5, the separate question of religious experience, Chapters 8–9 on morality and religious language, the gendered Father/Mother examples, and the five-tradition Chapter 1 context.
5. Prior mountain/team-spirit analogy risked presenting the contested nature of nonrealism as settled and reducing nonrealism to a psychological state. The new source-faithful comparison preserves its theological/semantic claim and the **conditional** nonbeliever consequence.
6. Earlier content and memory frames emphasized that philosophy's objective was to prevent arbitrary compartmentalization in **every** person's worldview. Kellenberger's stated claim is more precise: believers and nonbelievers have different reasons to examine religion; his final methodological claim is reciprocal conversation involving religious sensibilities, not a categorical verdict.
7. The supplied summary guides sometimes expanded formal argument types or supplied a modern metaphysics debate absent from these five source pages. Those additions were not attributed to Kellenberger.
