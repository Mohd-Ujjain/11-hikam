## Chapter 2 — “Proving God's Existence” (PDF 3–25)

| PDF page(s) | Source's argumentative unit | Fidelity requirements for 3.1.6 |
|---|---|---|
| **3–5** | Kellenberger distinguishes **whether a proof succeeds logically** from **whether a proof, successful or not, matters religiously**. His chapter addresses the former, postponing the latter to Chapter 3 (which is **not included** in the pack). The three traditional arguments employ natural reason rather than revelation. He teaches proposition/premise/conclusion, deduction, validity, soundness, begging the question, and induction. A conditional about trees and God is **valid but question-begging**; mere validity is insufficient. | Preserve the logical vocabulary exactly and distinguish the source's *tree example* from a genuine proof. Do not invent the missing Chapter 3 account of religious relevance. |
| **5–8** | Anselm's `Proslogion` takes a **conceptual / a priori** route: the being than which no greater can be conceived cannot exist only in the understanding if existing in reality would make it greater. The account is situated within Anselm's prayerful faith seeking understanding, not presented as a neutral historical claim that all believers share this proof. | Set out the source's actual premise → conclusion and distinguish faith's role in Anselm's own project from the logical evaluation of that reasoning. |
| **8–11** | Gaunilo offers the “Lost Island” parallel, to which Anselm objects that God and an island are logically unlike because the divine concept includes necessity. Kant's objection is that existence is not a predicate that augments a concept. Kellenberger notes a nuance in Kant's stronger generalization: even if necessary existence can be intelligibly part of a concept, that does not thereby demonstrate an *instance* of it. He also brings in Aquinas's distinction between what is self-evident in itself and what is evident to humans, and asks what characteristics/uniqueness the proof would establish. | Keep distinct the **Gaunilo counterexample**, **Anselm rejoinder**, **Kant predication criticism**, and **Kellenberger's qualification**; do not flatten them into “the ontological argument was refuted.” |
| **12–14** | Aquinas rejects a proof from our knowledge of God's essence but argues from observable **effects**. He offers five ways; their cosmological connections differ. The source gives special attention to motion/change, efficient causes, and contingency/necessity, including the asserted impossibility of an infinite causal regress and the identification of an ultimate necessary source. | Trace Aquinas's *different ways*, not one generic “everything has a cause” assertion. |
| **14–17** | Critical analysis examines the rejection of infinite regress, different kinds of regress and causal explanation, and the move from an explanatory principle to the personal/attribute-rich God of a tradition. Kellenberger distinguishes a **necessary being** or first cause proved in an argument (if proved) from additional claims about divine attributes. | Record the inferential gap without importing external criticism; do not ascribe all biblical or Islamic divine attributes to a proof that has not established them. |
| **18–20** | The teleological/design argument in Paley's version depends on observed purposive order and an **analogy** between artifacts and natural objects, illustrated by the famous watch. It is **inductive**, unlike the intended deductive force of the ontological and cosmological arguments. Paley goes beyond a designer to argue for attributes by observations of nature. | Teaching comparison must distinguish argument *form*, *starting evidence*, *type of conclusion*, and *claims about attributes*. |
| **20–22** | Hume's objections challenge how closely the world resembles a human artifact, the inference to an infinite/perfect creator from finite effects, and what the observed world could tell us about a cause's nature. The discussion further distinguishes design as purposive work from **adaptation** that may result through selection; the latter does not automatically imply a designer. | Attribute Hume's and related objections to their actual proponents and preserve Kellenberger's argumentative qualification; do not silently make an outside verdict. |
| **22–25** | “Summing Up and Going Further” recognizes each traditional argument's affinity with different aspects of religious sensibility while keeping their **logical strength under continuing discussion**. The chapter deliberately does **not** decide the religious-relevance question, since it is reserved for another chapter. | Preserve that unresolved ending, rather than forcing a conclusive proof or disproof. |

## Chapter 6 — “The Religious Problem of Evil” (PDF 26–38)

| PDF page(s) | Source's argumentative unit | Fidelity requirements for 3.1.6 |
|---|---|---|
| **26–27** | Kellenberger first distinguishes the **practical problem** (how to lessen evil in the world) from the **religious problem** (how suffering or evil is reconciled with faith in a good, powerful God). He uses the situation of a mother whose child has leukemia to show the religious problem as personal and not merely abstract. | Do not turn an invented exam example into the source's case or confuse the two problems. |
| **27–30** | The religious problem arises within commitments to God's goodness and power alongside the existence of evil. He sets out three propositions; differing faith traditions may not accept the same theological premises. The problem's urgency can exist *within faith*; he uses Ivan Karamazov to distinguish accepting a deity's existence from believing in divine moral order. The source distinguishes **moral evil** from **natural evil**. | Keep distinctions among theodicies, belief that God exists, faith in God, and traditions whose convictions differ. |
| **30–31** | A **theodicy** proposes a reason *actually* justifying the divine permission of evil; a **defense** needs to establish only a possible reason consistent with God's existence. The free-will proposal emphasizes human choosing wrongdoing, and includes traditional treatments of Adam/Eve, Augustine and Milton. | Do not collapse theodicy and defense or treat their same examples as identical logical claims. |
| **31–32** | Criticisms of free will challenge its handling of **natural evils** and ask about whether freedom requires the actual possibility or presence of wrongdoing, including the possibilities known to an omniscient creator. Responses and criticisms are presented in their original order. | Preserve the objections as objections and do not invent a final resolution. |
| **32** | Leibniz's **best of all possible worlds** proposal is presented and criticized, notably through Voltaire's skeptical response and the difficulty of showing that any particular suffering is necessary to a best world. | Distinguish satire from logical disproof (the source explicitly does). |
| **32–33** | Irenaean theodicy presents moral/spiritual **growth** as a reason for a world with both moral and natural evil; critics ask why perfection could not have been created from the start and how suffering serves that end. | Keep the Irenaean position and the author's unresolved criticisms in view. |
| **33–36** | “When the Religious Problem of Evil Is Not a Problem”: the Book of Job, the Psalmist's awareness of goodness, and an experiential attitude towards creation. Kellenberger explores a **conditional epistemic** argument: someone who truly knows divine goodness would not regard apparently contrary evil as evidence against it. He does not simply assert that all people have such knowledge; whether the experience really is knowledge is a further issue. | Do not misrepresent this as a universal dismissal of evil, an established proof, or the author's unqualified endorsement. |
| **36–38** | Returning to the **practical problem**, Kellenberger contrasts Ivan's moral protest with Alyosha's concrete care for children in Dostoevsky's story. Alyosha neither justifies children's suffering through a theodicy nor ignores it; his response addresses suffering directly. The conclusion maintains that whether a theodicy is required depends partly on the believer's cognitive position, and the questions remain open. | The Alyosha example is source-provided and specifically concerns the distinction between a speculative answer and a practical moral response. Do not convert it into a separate ChatGPT moral lesson. |

### Proposed pedagogy (not yet published)

For the source's three propositions, a precise concept-relationship or numbered logic schema is more useful than generic summary tables. A genuinely comparative table should contrast the **purpose, evil type addressed, and chief objection** for the free-will, Leibnizian and Irenaean approaches. A source-grounded local clarification can distinguish **theodicy vs defense**. The Ivan/Alyosha narrative is appropriate for a source-specific checkpoint and should not become a generic motivational analogy.
