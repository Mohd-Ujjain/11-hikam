## Scholarly thesis and degree of certainty

The author begins from the observable seams in the transmitted `Metaphysics`, rejects treating it as a single finished work, and simultaneously resists treating all its books as unrelated. Cross-book references and argument continuity support a **qualified core sequence**, reconstructed logically as **A–B–Γ–E–Z–H–Θ–M–N–I**. The author then distinguishes α, Δ, K and Λ for different reasons; argues for several local editorial insertions; and weighs limited, sometimes conflicting ancient evidence for editions, book counts, naming and manuscript rolls. This is a reconstruction based on unequal strengths of evidence. “Possible,” “perhaps,” “probably,” “suggest,” and “not conclusive” are meaningful source qualifications. The author sometimes opposes Jaeger's earlier arguments while explicitly reporting agreement with part of Jaeger's later reconstruction; the edition must not distort that relationship into uniform opposition.

## Editorial issues and safeguards

1. The public chapter credits **W. D. Ross** prominently and separately identifies Aristotle as the ancient author of the treatise being discussed. All ten Companion attribution records also credit Ross.
2. The supplementary “Total Recall” drafts emphasize philosophical doctrines that appear only tangentially in the primary introduction; they are **not** the intellectual source for 2.2.5.
3. The received order, the reconstructed ten-book teaching order, the inferred composition chronology, and the physical roll arrangement are **different kinds of order**, each with its own evidentiary limits.
4. α, Δ, K and Λ are not indiscriminately labeled forged. The source distinguishes genuine separate books from possible notes, and K’s two halves must be evaluated independently.
5. The later-section cutoff after the opening Greek texts on Socrates/Plato is not reconstructed from general knowledge.
